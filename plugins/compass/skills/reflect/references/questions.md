# Question Library

A bank of neutral, drilling questions organized by purpose. Load this when:

- The conversation is repeating the same question shapes (recycling produces shallow answers)
- A particular angle isn't working and a different one might
- The user's answer feels rehearsed and needs an angle they haven't prepared for

**Selection rule:** Pick the question that targets the *part* of the user's answer that felt thin, vague, or rehearsed — not the strongest part. Ask one. Wait.

**Phrasing rule:** Strip adjectives that imply a direction. "How do you feel about the simpler option?" is not neutral; "How do you feel about each option?" is.

---

## Definitional — clarify what words mean

These surface hidden criteria the user hasn't named.

- "When you say 'better,' compared to what?"
- "What would 'good enough' look like — concretely?"
- "If a stranger overheard 'I want X,' what do you think they'd assume X means?"
- "What's the smallest version of this that would still feel like a win?"
- "If you couldn't use the word [user's word], how would you describe it?"
- "What's a word you keep almost using and then not using?"

## Comparative — surface the real reference point

People compare against an unspoken baseline. Name it.

- "What are you measuring this against?"
- "If the alternative is 'do nothing,' how does this look?"
- "What was the version of this you considered six months ago — what changed?"
- "Is the comparison 'this vs. another option' or 'this vs. how things are now'?"
- "Who else considered this and chose differently — and why?"

## Tension — point at contradictions without judgment

When the user's words don't all line up, name the gap as a question.

- "Earlier you said X. This sounds closer to Y. Which is the real want?"
- "You're describing both 'I need this' and 'it's a luxury.' Both can be true — which one is louder right now?"
- "You said you want to decide quickly. You also said you want to be sure. Where's the trade?"
- "The case for it is [A]. The hesitation is [B]. What would have to be true for [B] to stop mattering?"
- "Two things you said feel like they're in tension: [X] and [Y]. Is that real, or am I misreading?"

## Stakes — calibrate the size of the decision

Surface what the user is actually risking, in either direction.

- "If you did nothing for six months, what's the actual cost?"
- "If you chose wrong, how would you find out — and how soon?"
- "What's the worst plausible outcome, and how recoverable is it?"
- "Is this a one-way door or a two-way door?"
- "What's the upside you're hoping for, in concrete terms?"
- "Would you regret the path not taken, or just adjust?"
- "Even if it didn't work out, would the decision still have been good given what you knew at the time?" *(Separates decision quality from outcome — Annie Duke. Useful when the user is rationalizing past decisions to avoid examining present ones, or when fear of bad outcomes is conflating with fear of bad decisions.)*

## Affective — get under reasoning

Reasons can be assembled to fit a feeling. Check the feeling.

- "When you imagine having decided, which version feels lighter?"
- "Which option are you arguing yourself into, and which are you arguing yourself out of?"
- "If you imagine doing it tomorrow, what's the first feeling that shows up?"
- "What would it feel like to decide and not tell anyone?"
- "When you talk about [option], your energy shifts. Notice anything?"
- "Is the feeling 'I want this' or 'I want to be the kind of person who wants this'?"

## Origin — whose preference is this

Many "preferences" are inherited, performed, or peer-driven.

- "Whose voice is that — yours, or someone else's?"
- "Where did you first hear this is something to want?"
- "If no one ever knew you'd done this, would you still want to?"
- "If [specific person] disagreed, would your answer change?"
- "Is this a preference, or a position you've been defending long enough that it feels like one?"

## Time horizon — collapse or expand the frame

Decisions feel different at different time scales.

- "What does this look like in five years?"
- "What does it look like next week?"
- "If you could only revisit this once a year, what would you decide?"
- "Is the urgency real, or is the urgency the discomfort of not deciding?"
- "How long has this been on your mind, and what's kept it open?"

## Identity — who is choosing

The chooser shapes the choice. Name the version of the user making the call.

- "Which version of you is making this decision — the tired one, the planning one, the curious one?"
- "Who is this for — present-you or future-you?"
- "If a friend you trusted described you choosing this, what would they say about you?"
- "Does this fit the life you're living, or the life you want to be living?"

## Counterfactual — flip the frame

Stress-test by inverting.

- "If you woke up tomorrow having already done it, what's your first thought?"
- "If you woke up tomorrow having decided not to, what's your first thought?"
- "Pretend you've already chosen. What would the next month look like?"
- "If someone gave this to you for free, would you take it? If they paid you to skip it, would you?"

## Premortem and Backcasting — imagine the future, then walk back

From Annie Duke's *Thinking in Bets*. Useful when the user is weighing a choice in the abstract and would benefit from imagining the concrete future.

**Premortem — surface unspoken risks:**

- "Imagine you've decided yes, and a year later it didn't work out. What happened?"
- "Imagine the version where this turned out to be a mistake. What was the mistake — was it the choice, the execution, or something outside your control?"
- "If a friend told you they'd done this and regretted it, what reason would feel most plausible?"

**Backcasting — surface what success actually looks like:**

- "Imagine you've decided yes, and a year later it's working beautifully. Walk me back from there. What was the first thing that went right?"
- "If this turned out to be one of the better decisions you've made, what made it so?"
- "Describe the version of you who has this and is glad they do. What's their day like?"

If both come back vague, that itself is information — the user may not have a clear picture of either failure or success.

## Question-the-question — examine the framing itself

Sometimes the question the user brought isn't the right question. These probe the framing without abandoning the conversation.

- "Is this the question you actually need to answer, or is the real question one level up?"
- "If this question were already answered, what would you be working on next? Is *that* the thing?"
- "You're framing this as a yes/no. Is that the right shape, or is it more like 'when' or 'how much'?"
- "If you imagine the version of you a year from now looking back, what question would they wish you'd asked instead?"
- "What's the question you've been avoiding asking?"

## Resource — name what's actually scarce

Constraints clarify priorities.

- "Which is scarcer for you right now — money, time, attention, or energy?"
- "What does this cost you in the resource you have least of?"
- "If you had twice as much of [resource], would the answer change?"

## Permission — when the user is seeking validation

If the user keeps reaching for the agent's opinion, redirect — don't supply it.

- "It sounds like you're looking for someone to say 'yes' or 'no.' Notice that. What would you do if no one weighed in?"
- "If I told you to do it, how would that feel? If I told you not to?"
- "Are you trying to decide, or trying to feel allowed to decide?"

## Closing — when sensing arrival

Use sparingly. Only when the user's words and tone suggest a position has formed.

- "Say it in one sentence."
- "If you wrote down where you've landed, would it still feel right tomorrow morning?"
- "Is there anything you'd want to add before we save this?"
- "Open or concluded — which one feels honest right now?"
